# Phase 3-B — Literature retrieval: how strong is "science-based lifting"?

Retrieval only. Grades: [A] meta-analysis/replicated RCT · [B] single good study/consistent observational · [C] mechanistic/small-n/expert consensus · [D] marketing/anecdote. Grade attaches to the claim, not the source — a meta-analysis of untrained-only studies gets a weaker grade for a trained-population claim.

| 1 | **Volume dose-response** (sets/muscle/wk → hypertrophy) | Schoenfeld/Ogborn/Krieger 2017: 15 studies, 34 arms. Pelland et al. 2025 (Sports Med, meta-regression): 67 studies, 2,058 participants (79% male), 220 hypertrophy + 490 strength effect sizes. | Individual trials mostly n=15–30/arm | Schoenfeld 2017 [A] (+0.38%/set, linear); Pelland 2025 [A] (posterior prob. slope>0 = 100%, diminishing returns); Baz-Valle 2022 systematic review (pubmed 35291645) | Mostly untrained/recreational; Pelland 2025 mixed, "young trained men" subgroup gives ~12–20 sets/wk optimum but that subgroup is a fraction of the 67 studies | Schoenfeld: 0.38%/set continuous slope, wide CIs; Pelland: 100% posterior probability of positive slope but strongly diminishing, high between-study heterogeneity noted in both | The "volume landmarks" (MV/MEV/MAV/MRV) taught by RP/Israetel and popularized by Nippard are a coaching heuristic layered on top of a linear/curvilinear meta-regression — the specific landmark numbers are not themselves tested in RCTs (inference). Individual-response heterogeneity: a 2025 bioRxiv within-participant unilateral replication trial highlights large between-individual variability in volume response, undercutting a single "optimal number" for everyone. |
| 2 | **Training frequency** (sessions/muscle/wk) | Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019 meta-analysis: 25 studies | n mostly 10–24/study | Schoenfeld/Grgic/Krieger 2019 [A] | Mixed trained/untrained | No significant difference 1 vs 2 vs 3+ /wk once volume is equated; modest effect favoring higher frequency only in non-volume-equated meta-regression | "Strong evidence frequency doesn't matter independent of volume" is a genuinely well-replicated finding — one of the more settled corners of the field |
| 3 | **Proximity to failure / RIR** | Robinson et al. 2024 (Sports Med) meta-regression: 55 hypertrophy studies + 67 strength studies. Refalo et al. 2023 (updated continuous RIR modelling). Earlier Grgic 2021 failure-vs-non-failure meta. | n commonly 12–30/arm | Robinson 2024 [A], Refalo 2023 [A] | Robinson/Refalo pooled populations mixed trained/untrained; ACSM 2026 overview states "completing sets to fatigue does not enhance gains" | Hypertrophy slope positive but flattening past ~2 RIR (i.e., some benefit to training close to failure, but diminishing); strength shows negligible difference by proximity | Live dispute: RP/Israetel historically pushed training-to-failure emphasis; newer meta-regressions (Robinson/Refalo 2024) and ACSM 2026 lean toward "1–3 RIR sufficient, true failure not required" — a case where earlier practitioner consensus has been walked back by the newest, better-powered synthesis |
| 4 | **Periodization** (periodized vs non-periodized, volume-equated) | Grgic et al. 2017/2018 meta-analyses (hypertrophy: PeerJ 2017 linear vs undulating; separate periodized-vs-non meta for strength) | n typically 15–30/study | Grgic 2017 [A] (hypertrophy: no sig. difference LP vs DUP); other Grgic meta [A] (strength: periodized > non-periodized) | Mixed; ACSM 2026 overview states impact of periodization on strength "could not be determined" from current evidence — signals unresolved | For hypertrophy specifically: no significant advantage found for periodized vs non-periodized or LP vs DUP when volume equated | This is one of the thinner topics — the ACSM's own 2026 overview-of-reviews says periodization's effect "could not be determined," despite periodization being taught as a near-doctrinal pillar of "science-based" programming (inference: consensus outruns evidence here) |
| 5 | **Exercise selection / ROM / lengthened-position training** | Multiple systematic reviews; recent meta (2024) pooling 24 studies on partial vs full ROM; Wolf et al. multi-site replication 2025 (PeerJ) | Older studies n=10–20; Wolf 2025 is a genuine multi-site (15 sites) pre-registered replication — much larger, stronger design | 2024 meta: 24 studies, SMD=0.12 [-0.02, 0.26] trivial difference full ROM vs partial; Wolf 2023 earlier meta suggested small lengthened-partial advantage; Wolf 2025 [A] multi-site pre-registered contradicts the earlier small advantage, finding comparable arm/thigh hypertrophy | Wolf 2025 explicitly trained individuals, multi-site — one of the few genuinely well-powered, pre-registered, trained-population RT trials in the field | Trivial-to-small effect, wide CI crossing zero in the 2024 pooled meta | Direct within-topic replication failure: Wolf 2023's "lengthened partials win" finding did not hold up in Wolf 2025's own larger pre-registered multi-site follow-up — a rare, citable instance of a specific claim being retracted/downgraded by better-powered replication |
| 6 | **Protein total daily dose** | Morton et al. 2018 meta-analysis/meta-regression: 49 trials, 1,863 participants | Individual trials mostly n=20–40/arm | Morton 2018 [A] — plateau at ~1.6 g/kg/day (95% CI 1.03–2.20, i.e. wide) | Healthy adults, mixed trained/untrained, resistance-trained subgroup smaller | Mean FFM effect of protein supplementation ≈ +0.30 kg over control; plateau CI is wide enough that true plateau could be anywhere ~1.0–2.2 g/kg | One of the better-evidenced pillars (large N relative to the field, real meta-regression with a dose-response curve) but the wide CI on the plateau point is rarely mentioned when "1.6 g/kg" is quoted as a hard number |
| 7 | **Protein per-meal distribution / leucine threshold** | Multi-level meta-regression (protein timing): 23 studies, 525 subjects, 132 effect sizes (hypertrophy). Areta et al. 2013 acute single-study leucine/MPS work underlies the "20-25g threshold" claim | n commonly 10–20/arm for distribution-specific RCTs | Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018 (JISSN) is a **narrative review**, not a meta-analysis, despite being cited as authoritative for "0.4g/kg per meal, 3-4x/day"; separate timing meta-analyses [A] found no significant hypertrophy benefit from specific meal timing | Mixed; leucine-threshold mechanism (Areta 2013) comes from acute MPS studies in a handful of subjects, not chronic hypertrophy RCTs | Timing meta-analyses report no significant hypertrophy effect of distribution pattern; per-meal "20-25g threshold" is extrapolated from acute 3-6h MPS assays, not from long-term body-composition outcomes | Textbook example of mechanism-outrunning-outcome-evidence: the specific "25g / 0.4g per kg per meal, every 3-4h" numbers trace back to short-term MPS studies and a review article, not to a hypertrophy-outcome meta-analysis confirming that distribution pattern changes real muscle gain |
| 8 | **Energy balance / rate of gain for lean bulking** | Small literature. Garthe et al. 2013 (athletes, structured vs ad-lib surplus); Helms et al. 2023 (Sports Med Open) parallel-groups small vs large surplus | Garthe: modest n (structured cohort, tens of athletes); Helms 2023: **21 randomized, 17 completers** (COVID dropout) | No meta-analysis identified — narrative/individual-trial level only | Both trained/competitive-athlete populations — actually a rare case where the population is well-matched to real users | Garthe: structured (~2.4 g/kg protein, ~500kcal surplus) group gained more total weight but proportionally more fat; Helms 2023: no significant strength/muscle advantage of 15% vs 5% surplus over 8 weeks, small n limits power | This entire topic rests on a literature of single-digit RCT count with n in the teens-to-twenties and 8-week durations — the "0.25-0.5%bodyweight/week lean bulk" guidance widely taught by coaches is not backed by a meta-analysis; it is inference from 2-3 small trials plus bioenergetics modelling |
| 9 | **Rest interval length** | Grgic et al. 2017 systematic review; newer Bayesian meta-analysis (Frontiers 2024) | n typically 10-20/arm | Grgic 2017 [B/A borderline, review not full meta]; 2024 Bayesian meta [A] — modest hypertrophy benefit for >60s rest, no further benefit >90s | Grgic 2017 explicitly notes advantage for longer rest is clearer in trained vs untrained lifters — one of few topics with a stated trained-vs-untrained split in the source itself | Small hypertrophy benefit (>60s vs <60s), plausibly mediated by higher volume load rather than rest per se; ACSM 2026 overview says short vs long rest showed **no impact on strength** | Reasonably converged: modest, mechanistically-explained (volume load) effect, not a live fight in the field |

### Additional topic volunteered: measurement/assessment validity underlying all of the above
Ultrasound muscle-thickness measurement error (CV ~0.5–17.9%, weighted average ~6.5%) is frequently **comparable in magnitude to the actual hypertrophy signal being measured** in short trials, per a 2024 Sports Medicine – Open methods paper. This is a cross-cutting caveat on every hypertrophy outcome cited above, not a separate lifting topic, but it materially weakens confidence in point estimates from any single small trial.

## Methodological quality of the field (meta-level findings)

| Issue | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Small samples baked into old guidelines | Prior ACSM frequency guidance was "informed by only three studies (n = 59 total participants), and two of the studies did not investigate frequency" | ACSM 2026 position-stand overview of reviews (PMC12965823) |
| General RCT quality in the field | "Randomized trials in sports and exercise medicine-related fields are often poor quality with small sample sizes, poor randomization methodology, lack of preregistration, poor reporting of adverse events" | ACSM 2026 overview |
| Scale of aggregation vs individual trial size | The 2026 ACSM overview pools 137 systematic reviews (>30,000 participants total) — but that aggregate masks that each underlying primary RCT is still typically n=10-40 | ACSM 2026 overview |
| Trained vs untrained mismatch | Most classic hypertrophy RCTs (Mitchell 2014, many volume/frequency trials) use previously untrained young men over 12-16 weeks; genuinely trained-population, well-powered trials (Wolf 2025 ROM replication, Helms 2023 surplus study) are recent, rare, and still small (n=17-30ish) | Mitchell et al. 2014 (J Appl Physiol); Wolf et al. 2025 (PeerJ); Helms et al. 2023 (Sports Med Open) |
| Surrogate-measure problem | Acute (3-6h) myofibrillar protein synthesis does NOT correlate with 16-week chronic hypertrophy outcome in the same men (Mitchell et al. 2014) — yet acute MPS studies still underpin nutrition-timing claims (e.g., leucine threshold, anabolic window) | Mitchell et al. 2014, J Appl Physiol; general MPS-vs-hypertrophy controversy summarized in IJSNEM 2021 "Making Sense of Muscle Protein Synthesis" |
| Measurement error vs signal size | Ultrasound muscle-thickness error can approach or exceed the true training-induced change within a typical 5-13 week study window (change ~7-31% depending on muscle/duration vs CV up to ~18%) | Sports Medicine – Open 2024 methods paper on ultrasound measurement error |
| Short study durations | Commonly-cited hypertrophy trials run 5-13 weeks; almost none run beyond 16-24 weeks, so "long-term" claims are extrapolated from short trials | Cross-cutting observation across all trials cited above (inference from the individual study durations retrieved) |
| Individual-response variability | A 2025 bioRxiv replicated within-participant unilateral trial found substantial individual-level heterogeneity in hypertrophic response to volume — a single population-average dose-response curve may not represent any given individual well | bioRxiv 2025.07.24.666533 (inference: single-study, not yet peer-reviewed at time of retrieval) |
| Live, unresolved disputes | (a) Volume "landmarks"/junk-volume framing (Israetel/RP, Nippard) is a coaching heuristic on top of, not confirmed by, the linear/curvilinear meta-regressions; (b) failure-training doctrine is being walked back by newer meta-regressions/ACSM 2026; (c) periodization's hypertrophy benefit is explicitly "could not be determined" by ACSM 2026 despite being taught as settled | See rows 1, 3, 4 above |
