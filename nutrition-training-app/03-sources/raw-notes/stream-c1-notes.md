## 0. Headline methodological finding: a fabricated-looking citation, caught and rejected

Search for "single interface vs separate apps calorie deficit adherence" surfaced, near the top of results, this claim from a diet/exercise app's own marketing blog (Nutrola, 2026):

> "A 2023 study published in *Obesity Reviews* found that people who tracked both diet and exercise in a single interface were 47% more likely to maintain a calorie deficit over 12 weeks compared to those using separate apps."

This is **exactly** the claim the whole project needs — which is itself a reason to distrust it. WebFetch of the source page found **no DOI, no author names, no issue number, nothing to verify**. Searching for the claim by any combination of terms returns only the same blog post and its syndication, never a journal record, an abstract, or a citing paper. *Obesity Reviews* is a real journal, which makes this worse, not better — it borrows the credibility of a real venue for an unfindable article. This has the signature of an LLM- or copywriter-generated statistic invented to sound authoritative for content marketing.

**Treated as: not evidence. Not tagged [D] (a [D] tag implies a real-but-weak source). Tagged as unverifiable/likely-fabricated and excluded.** Recorded here so the architect knows it was checked and rejected, not missed — and as a live example of the laundering risk the whole Wave 1 mandate exists to catch (Phase 1 did the milder version of this: citing real industry blogs for numbers, not inventing a fake journal citation).

## 1. Primary question — one app vs two apps for adherence

**Direct test: not found.** Searched multiple phrasings (multi-behaviour intervention adherence single vs separate tools; integrated diet+exercise app vs separate apps RCT; app consolidation adherence; mHealth app consolidation retention). No RCT, cohort study, or controlled comparison exists that tests "one logging app covering N behaviours" against "N separate logging apps" and measures adherence/retention as the outcome, for any behaviour pair (diet+exercise or otherwise). This is a true absence, not a search failure — the literature that exists is all adjacent:

- **Multiple Health Behaviour Change (MHBC) intervention literature** (Prochaska school) compares *simultaneous vs sequential delivery of coaching/counselling targeting 2+ behaviours* — not app count. A systematic review of RCTs found simultaneous and sequential approaches "should be considered equally efficacious," with weak/sparse evidence that sequential smoking-cessation-first designs may do better for smoking specifically, and that sequential designs may cost more delivery time and *reduce participant adherence* simply by running longer (ScienceDirect, "Comparative efficacy of simultaneous versus sequential MHBC interventions," systematic review of RCTs). [B] — but the variable under test is behaviour-sequencing, not tool/app count, and none of these are UK strength+nutrition studies. Relevance to the primary question: weak, adjacent.
- **"App fatigue" (Ali & Thu, 2025, *PLOS Digital Health*, 4(11):e0001107)** names the exact intuition the project needs — "the cumulative sense of burden, disengagement, and cognitive exhaustion that arises from managing too many disconnected tools" (>350,000 health apps exist; ~90,000 released in 2020 alone) — and explicitly proposes consolidation as a partial remedy. But the authors themselves write that **"systematic evidence on its effects on trust, engagement, and health outcomes is still limited."** A paper naming the phenomenon and citing no controlled study measuring it is itself evidence of the gap, not a resolution of it. [C] concept description, explicitly not [A/B] evidence.
- **Treatment-burden literature (multimorbidity)**: consistent finding that patients managing multiple self-care regimens for multiple *chronic conditions* report higher burden and worse adherence, and some patients "intentionally do not adhere... to alleviate their treatment burden" (multiple PMC reviews on hypertension+comorbidity self-care, treatment-burden PROMs). [B] as observational evidence — but population is multimorbid chronic-disease patients managing *distinct medical regimens* (meds, glucose checks, BP checks), not a healthy lifter juggling two lifestyle-logging apps. Extrapolating this to "two logging apps for one healthy person" is a real population/task mismatch (per evidence-standards rule 7) — drops at least one grade when applied here. Tag the extrapolation [C], not [B].
- **General cognitive-science context-switching cost**: workplace/productivity literature (not health-specific) shows measurable resumption costs after interruption (Carnegie Mellon-derived findings: brief interruptions can increase task-completion time up to 23%; "attention residue" concept). This is real cognitive psychology but entirely about knowledge-work task-switching, not about opening a second logging app once a day. [C] mechanistic analogy at best; most of the widely-quoted numbers here ("$450B/year," "89 apps per company") are corporate-productivity-vendor content (Asana, BasicOps, Waymaker), [D], and not health-app relevant at all — noted so they are NOT accidentally imported into the product doc as if health-specific.
- **Multi-metric self-tracking (single tool, multiple health dimensions)**: one study found self-recording across multiple health metrics *in one system* raised lifestyle-awareness more for physical activity (45%) and nutrition (52%) than for sleep (13%) or stress (2%) — interesting because it's a single-tool multi-behaviour design, but it measures *awareness*, not adherence/retention, and has no separate-apps comparison arm. [C], tangential.

**Conclusion for this section, stated plainly per the standards' "absence of evidence is a finding" rule: no study, of any grade, directly tests whether consolidating training and nutrition logging into one app produces higher adherence than using two separate apps.** The closest adjacent literatures (app fatigue, treatment burden, context-switching) are all consistent with the *hypothesis* being plausible, and none contradict it — but none test it either. The one source that appeared to test it directly was an unverifiable, likely-fabricated marketing statistic (see §0) and must not be used. This is carried into both deliverable files as the load-bearing finding.

## 2. Retention curves — sources found, by grade

**[A]-grade (best available replacement for Phase 1's [D] figures):**
- Kidman PG, Curtis RG, Watson A, Maher CA. "When and Why Adults Abandon Lifestyle Behavior and Mental Health Mobile Apps: Scoping Review." *J Med Internet Res* 2024;26:e56897. doi:10.2196/56897. Scoping review, 18 peer-reviewed studies (2014–2022), pooled N=525,824 (individual study N from 10 to 189,770).
  - Segmented abandonment rates: physical activity apps 54–75%; diet apps 86%; mental-health apps 89–92%; alcohol apps 95–97%; smoking-cessation apps 40%.
  - Pooled: median 70% of users discontinued within first 100 days.
  - Curve shape: curvilinear — sharp early abandonment, then a slowing rate ("two theorized periods": immediate deterrents vs gradual disengagement).
  - Note: this review itself cites an industry figure inside it ("66% of health apps and 69% of fitness apps abandoned within 90 days," attributed to an Alchemer healthcare-apps benchmark) — that specific number stays [D] regardless of appearing inside an [A] review; the review's own 18-study synthesis is the [A] part.

**[B]-grade (rigorous methodology, adjacent domain):**
- Baumel A, Muench F, Edan S, Kane JM. "Objective User Engagement With Mental Health Apps: Systematic Search and Panel-Based Usage Analysis." *J Med Internet Res* 2019;21(9):e14567. Real usage-panel analytics (not self-report/survey) across a large sample of mental-health apps. Finding: 30-day retention for popular mental-health apps as low as ~3%, DAU ~4%. Not nutrition/fitness, but methodologically important: it is one of very few academic studies using *actual analytics data* rather than self-report or industry marketing, and its numbers are considerably worse than the self-report-survey-based figures common in industry blogs — suggesting analytics-grade nutrition/fitness numbers, if they existed, would likely also undercut the more optimistic industry figures.
- Chiu YC et al. (title: "Assessing User Retention of a Mobile App: Survival Analysis," *JMIR mHealth uHealth* 2020;8(11):e16309). Survival analysis of a GPS-based work-hours logging app, N=342 (trainee physicians + others). Passive (automatic) data retention >> active (user-entered) data retention; 1-week retention Android 69.2% vs iOS 36.2% for passive data; both crash toward 0–5% by 1 month. Not fitness/nutrition, but directly relevant methodologically: even in a captive, motivated, professional sample, *active user-entered logging* retention collapses within weeks — a structural argument, not an anecdote, for why manual food/lift logging is intrinsically hard to sustain regardless of app quality.

**[D]-grade industry figures found (named explicitly, per standards rule — never load-bearing):**
- Business of Apps health & fitness app benchmarks (2026 edition) — WebFetch blocked (403); could not independently verify methodology. Secondary blogs quoting it (Sendbird, GetStream, Lucid, RetentionCheck, ApsteQ, enable3) converge on: fitness apps ~31% 90-day retention; Day 1 ~20–27%, Day 7 ~7%, Day 30 ~3%; annual subscription retention ~33%. None of these blogs publish raw methodology, sample composition, or a citable underlying dataset — they cite each other. Treat as a single [D] data lineage repeated many times, not independent corroboration.
- Named product anecdotes: Strava 90-day retention 18%→32% after a "Challenges" feature (2022); Down Dog +20% 90-day retention after adding flexible weekly goals. Single-company, unaudited, marketing-adjacent claims. [D].

**Honest gap statement:** No peer-reviewed, analytics-based (not self-report), day-30/day-90 retention study specific to *strength-training log* apps (Hevy/Strong-type) or *general diet-tracking* apps (MyFitnessPal-type) was found. Searches for "MyFitnessPal retention study," "Hevy/Strong churn statistics," and "workout app retention academic" returned only UX case studies, marketing/retention-strategy blog posts, or validity studies (measuring nutrient-estimate accuracy, not retention). **This specific gap — no primary-sourced retention curve exists for either half of this app's target domain — should be stated in the product document as an unresolved limitation, not silently patched with an industry-blog number presented as authoritative.**

## 3. Abandonment causes by segment — sources

- Kidman et al. 2024 (as above): taxonomy of 6 categories / 22 reasons — technical/functional; privacy; poor UX; content/features gap; time/financial cost (burden); evolving needs and goals (which bundles goal-achievement, motivation loss, and app-switching together — the review does not further split these three apart quantitatively).
- Mustafa AS, Ali N, Dhillon JS, et al. "User Engagement and Abandonment of mHealth: A Cross-Sectional Survey." *Healthcare (Basel)* 2022;10(2):221. Survey, N=209 mHealth users, Sept 2020–Jan 2021. Abandonment reasons: loss of interest/declining motivation 31.6%; frequent app-switching 21.5%; absence of desired features 18.7%; not enjoyable 10%; difficult usability 8.6%. Segmented heavy users (n=51, daily+) vs light users (n=41, occasional/lapsed) — both groups wanted personalization, food tracking, gamified features, feedback (i.e., segment didn't change *stated preferences* much, only usage frequency).
- Attig C, Franke T. "Abandonment of personal quantification: A review and empirical study investigating reasons for wearable activity tracking attrition." *Computers in Human Behavior* 2020;102:223–237. N=159 former wearable users. Key distinction: **perceived data inaccuracy/uselessness** and **loss of tracking motivation** were the two dominant drivers; *permanent* abandonment specifically linked to motivation loss, distinguishing it from temporary lapses (which have other causes). This is one of very few sources that separates temporary lapse from permanent quit as different phenomena with different drivers.
- Telenutrition dropout qualitative study (PMC11671310): small pilot, dropouts reported 60% marital/family-responsibility disruption, 50% goal not achieved, 50% work/school pressure (categories overlap, multiple reasons per person). [C], small-n.
- Young Adults' fitness-tracker qualitative study (PMC11555328): three segments — active users, former users, non-users — with former users citing anxiety about goal accomplishment, guilt/shame from broken streaks, and loss-of-motivation as central; non-users citing cost and privacy up front.

**Gap:** No study explicitly isolates **"plateau demotivation"** as its own labelled category distinct from general "motivation loss" — every taxonomy found bundles it into a broader motivation/goal-evolution bucket. Treat "plateau demotivation" specifically as inference from the general motivation-loss literature, not as an independently evidenced category.

## 4. Precision-vs-adherence tradeoff — verification of Phase 1's claim

Phase 1 claim (from `03-sources/raw-notes/phase1/constraints.md`): "5 RCTs of simplified vs detailed dietary self-monitoring, 4/5 equivalent weight loss; pilot RCT ~equal 5% weight-loss rates (43.2% vs 42.9%) and higher engagement in simplified arm."

**Verified independently, matches:**
- Nezami BT et al. "A pilot randomized trial of simplified versus standard calorie dietary self-monitoring in a mobile weight loss intervention." *Obesity* 2022;30(3). N=72 parents of young children, 6-month mobile behavioural weight-loss intervention. **Confirmed exact numbers**: simplified arm (tracked only high-calorie "red" foods) lost 4.0% body weight vs standard arm (full calorie tracking) 5.7%, not significantly different; rate reaching clinically meaningful ≥5% weight loss: 42.9% (simplified) vs 43.2% (standard) — Phase 1's specific figures are accurate to this specific study.
- A second, independent RCT found in the same space: detailed-vs-simplified self-monitoring in a digital weight-loss intervention among racial/ethnic-minority adults (*JMIR Formative Research* 2022;6(12):e42191, also reported PMC9795401). Detailed arm (all foods/drinks via Fitbit app) vs simplified arm (only "red zone" foods via web checklist). Self-monitoring adherence: median 49% of days (detailed) vs 97% of days (simplified) — a very large engagement gap. Weight change: −3.4 kg (detailed) vs −3.3 kg (simplified) — essentially equivalent despite the adherence gap.
- Broader systematic review, larger and more general than "5 RCTs": Raber M, Liao Y, Rara A, et al. "A systematic review of the use of dietary self-monitoring in behavioural weight loss interventions: delivery, intensity and effectiveness." *Public Health Nutrition* 2021;24(17):5885–5913. 59 total weight-loss studies (44 requiring monitoring of all intake, 15 requiring abbreviated/partial monitoring). 61% of full-monitoring studies showed significant weight loss vs control; 67% of abbreviated-monitoring studies did. Authors' own words: "lower and higher intensity dietary self-monitoring may support weight loss" — comparable effectiveness — but they flag that "variability in adherence measures and limited analysis of weight loss relative to self-monitoring usage limits our understanding," i.e., the field has not cleanly isolated intensity as the variable across all 59 studies.

**Could not independently verify:** the specific "5 RCTs, 4/5 equivalent" tally as a discrete, named meta-analytic count — I could not locate the exact review that reports exactly 5 RCTs with a 4/5 split (my last search on this exact point was cut off by the session's web-search budget cap). What I can state with confidence: (a) the *pattern* Phase 1 describes is real and replicates across at least 2 independently-located RCTs plus a 59-study systematic review, (b) the specific numbers Phase 1 quoted for "the pilot RCT" match Nezami et al. 2022 exactly, so that citation is solid, (c) the precise "5 RCTs" tally is plausible but unconfirmed as a discrete count — flag as *probably accurate but not independently re-derived this round*.

**Counter-evidence search:** No RCT found showing detailed/precise logging *outperforming* simplified logging on weight-loss outcome when both are followed. The one nuance that could be mistaken for counter-evidence: a cited 2016 meta-analysis finding that "greater adherence to dietary self-monitoring [associated with] more weight loss" — but this measures adherence-to-whatever-method-assigned, not precision-of-method, and the source explicitly flags it as confounded by participant motivation ("may be confounded by increased individual motivation to practice self-monitoring and a coinciding motivation to utilise other self-regulatory behaviours"). This is not counter-evidence to Phase 1's claim; if anything it reinforces it (sustained logging of any granularity beats abandoned precise logging) while adding an honest confound caveat relevant to §8 below.

## 5. Habit formation

- Lally P, van Jaarsveld CHM, Potts HWW, Wardle J. "How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world." *European Journal of Social Psychology* 2010;40(6):998–1009. N=96, self-chosen daily eating/drinking/activity behaviour, 12 weeks, daily Self-Report Habit Index. Median time to 95% of asymptotic automaticity: 66 days (range 18–254). A single missed day did not materially derail the automaticity trajectory. Complex behaviours reach a *lower* automaticity ceiling and take differently than simple ones (simple actions like "drink water" plateau faster/higher than elaborate routines like "50 sit-ups"). [A] — foundational, widely replicated in spirit though the exact 66-day median is frequently mis-cited as a fixed rule (it's a median of a wide, skewed range).
- Applying this to logging specifically is **(inference)**: Lally's study did not include a logging/self-tracking behaviour among its examples. The reasonable extrapolation — low-friction logging actions (single tap, photo) are more likely to reach automaticity than high-friction ones (multi-field gram-level entry) — follows from the "complex behaviours plateau lower" finding but was not tested on logging directly.
- Gollwitzer PM, Sheeran P. "Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes." *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 2006;38:69–119. d=0.65 (medium-large), 94 independent tests, general goal-attainment domain (not logging-specific). A large 2024 update (Sheeran, Listrom, Gollwitzer) reanalysed 642 independent tests and reframes the effect as contingent on plan format and motivational context rather than a single universal value — i.e., the 2006 headline number is real but the 2024 update is more cautious about a single effect size.
- One study links self-monitoring diaries directly to implementation-intention mechanics: a cue-monitoring + implementation-intentions RCT for unhealthy snacking (N=161) found that **keeping a self-monitoring/cue diary alone** reduced the target behaviour, with **no additional benefit** from layering formal if-then planning on top, at least short-term. This is directly relevant: it suggests the act of logging may already do much of the self-regulatory work that implementation-intention theory attributes to explicit if-then plans — but it's one study, short-term follow-up only. [B], single study.
- **Gap:** no study found that tests implementation intentions as an intervention specifically to improve *retention of the logging behaviour itself* (e.g., "if it's 8pm, then I open the app and log dinner") in a nutrition or fitness tracking app. This is inference bridging two adjacent literatures, not a direct finding.

## 6. Gamification — evidence for, and the case against

**For (short-term engagement):**
- Xu L et al. "The Effects of mHealth-Based Gamification Interventions on Participation in Physical Activity: Systematic Review." *JMIR mHealth uHealth* 2022;10(2):e27794. 50 studies (from 2,944 screened). Step counts (23 studies): 50% of controlled studies positive, 44% no significant difference. Overall PA time: 75% of single-group (non-controlled) studies improved, controlled-study results inconsistent. Most-used elements: goal-setting (60%), progress bars (52%), rewards (50%), points (44%), feedback (42%) — combinations outperform single elements. **Critical limitation stated by authors: 76% of included studies had no follow-up period at all** beyond the intervention itself — durability is largely untested, and where tested, effects are described as generally short-term. [A] by study count, but the durability conclusion is necessarily weak given the missing-follow-up problem.
- A 2025 meta-analysis specific to children/adolescents (*JMIR Serious Games* 2025;e68151) reports the opposite emphasis: effects "persist after follow-up... not solely due to novelty." **Different population** (children/adolescents, not adults) — per evidence-standards rule 7, this cannot be used to override the adult-population finding above; it should be reported as a population-specific counter-note, not blended into a general "gamification works long-term" claim.
- A gamification-in-education meta-analysis (different domain entirely, cited here only as a caution) found interventions run over multiple years were *adversely* associated with behaviour change — i.e., in that domain, longevity of the game layer backfired. Cross-domain, [C] at best, flagged as suggestive not probative for health apps.

**Against (mechanism — self-determination theory / overjustification):**
- Deci EL, Koestner R, Ryan RM. "A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation." *Psychological Bulletin* 1999;125(6):627–668. 128 studies. Engagement-contingent, completion-contingent, and performance-contingent rewards significantly undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28 respectively) and, for tangible/expected rewards specifically, also undermined self-reported interest (d = −0.15 to −0.17). [A] — this is the load-bearing citation for "the case against."
- Applying this to app streaks/points/badges specifically is **(inference)**: streaks and points are structurally engagement-contingent and completion-contingent reward schedules, which the meta-analysis found to be among the *most* undermining reward types for tasks people already find interesting. The bridge from "128 lab/field experiments on general tasks" to "logging behaviour in a phone app" is reasonable but not directly tested — grade the applied claim [B/C], not [A], per the standards' grade-inflation-check rule.

**Net verdict:** gamification produces real, measurable short-term engagement/activity gains in roughly half of controlled adult trials, with the evidence base for *durable* (post-intervention) behaviour change thin-to-absent for adults specifically (76% of the largest review's studies never even measured it). The SDT/overjustification mechanism gives a specific theoretical reason to expect exactly this pattern — a short-term bump followed by no advantage, or erosion, once the reward schedule stops or the novelty fades — but no study directly measured the *erosion* mechanism in a logging app. This is a coherent, converging picture, not a single knockout study.

## 7. Streak mechanics and documented harms

- "Abstinence violation effect" / "what-the-hell effect" (Marlatt & Gordon's relapse-prevention literature, addiction psychology) is a well-established clinical concept: after perceiving a lapse against a personal abstinence goal, people are prone to abandon the goal altogether rather than resume moderate adherence. [B/C] in its original (addiction, and — directly relevant, though not re-verified this session due to search-budget exhaustion — Herman & Polivy's disinhibition/counter-regulation research on restrained eaters, which is the specific dieting-domain version of this same mechanism). **This session found only product/growth-blog applications of the concept to app streak design specifically** (e.g., "streak collapses... often causing complete abandonment of the product," "perfectionism mindset where anything less than a perfect streak feels like total failure") — these are [D], not independently verified against a controlled study of streak mechanics in a logging app. No controlled study was found that quantifies a "streak-loss abandonment cascade" in a health/fitness app specifically.
- All-or-nothing / dichotomous thinking is a named cognitive distortion in CBT frameworks generally (Beck) — general mechanistic support [C] for why streak breaks might trigger disproportionate disengagement, again not tested in-app.
- **Net:** the mechanism (WTHE / abstinence-violation) is real and well-established in its home domain (addiction and, historically, restrained-eating research — directly on point for a nutrition-logging app). Its application to *app streak UI specifically* is currently supported only by plausible extrapolation and product-blog anecdote, not by a dedicated empirical study. This should be stated as: mechanism [B/C] in its original domain, application to app streaks (inference).

## 8. Social features

- "The code of sustainable success in fitness apps: social comparison mechanism enabled by user facilitated supports" (*Frontiers in Public Health*, 2025, PMC12358456). Single study. Upward comparison → increased self-efficacy, exercise/fitness interest, continuous-use intention. Downward comparison → decreased self-efficacy, decreased social presence, decreased attitude toward exercise. [B].
- Self-control as moderator (separate finding, same literature cluster): upward comparison improves wellbeing for *high* self-control users but *reduces* wellbeing for *low* self-control users — comparison direction's effect is not uniform across users. [B], single study.
- Public commitment: general social-psychology literature (college-student goal studies) shows public/witnessed commitment increases effort, persistence, and goal-consistent behaviour vs private commitment. [B], consistent across cited studies though not health-app-specific in all cases. A systematic review of commitment-contract effects on weight-loss/behaviour-change interventions found commitment devices worked in individually-delivered or group settings but *not* alongside minimal-contact delivery, and that *who* co-signs matters (peers or the intervention-deliverer produced the best effect, more than a generic app-only pledge would). [B].
- "Sticky Goals" (CHI 2021, large-scale stickK dataset, N=75,828 goals, 62.4% distinct users). Mixed financial+social commitment configurations outperformed either alone; "reputational stake" (a named friend-referee) functioned as a real psychological lever on completion. [B], large-N observational (not RCT).
- Leaderboard/cohort-design claim ("18% increase in lapsed users vs 31% retention lift, entirely driven by cohort-design choice") traced to a product/growth blog, not a peer-reviewed source — [D], included only as a design caution, not as evidence.

**Net:** direction and framing of social comparison matter more than whether social features exist at all. Upward, achievable, small-cohort comparison plus a real named human witness to a commitment tends to help; downward comparison, unreachable-top-performer leaderboards, and body/weight-based comparison tend to hurt — the last point is already independently established in Phase 1's ED harm-reduction findings (weight/shape leaderboards killed on ED grounds) and this section's findings are consistent with, not contradictory to, that kill decision.

## 9. Self-monitoring reactivity (does tracking cause the outcome, or proxy for motivation)

- Classical behaviour-therapy literature (McFall 1970 on smoking; Gottman & McFall 1972; theoretical reviews by Kanfer, and by Nelson & Hayes 1981) establishes **reactivity of self-monitoring** as a real, replicated phenomenon across decades of (mostly small-n, single-subject-design) studies: the act of recording a behaviour changes its frequency, independent of any external feedback, goal, or reward. Three competing mechanisms proposed (self-evaluation/self-consequences; cueing of ultimate environmental consequences; the whole monitoring process prompting external consequences) — mechanism disputed, phenomenon itself widely replicated. [B/C]: consistent small-n replication over 50 years, but methodologically dated (mostly single-subject designs, not large modern RCTs).
- Countervailing point, found in the precision/adherence literature above (§4): a cited 2016 meta-analysis finding that *adherence to self-monitoring* correlates with *more* weight loss explicitly flags that this may be **confounded by participant motivation** — i.e., people who log more consistently may simply be the more motivated people, not people who lost weight *because* they logged.
- **Net, stated honestly: both mechanisms are almost certainly operating simultaneously, and the literature says so explicitly rather than picking one.** Self-monitoring has a genuine, mechanistically-argued causal/reactive effect on behaviour (old but consistent small-n evidence), AND it is also a marker/proxy for underlying motivation that would partly predict the outcome regardless (the confound named directly in the weight-loss self-monitoring literature). No single study found that cleanly separates the two contributions for diet/exercise logging specifically — this is worth stating as a limitation rather than resolved.
